*In 2019, Google's semi-secret recruiting challenge surfaced in my browser. I froze, overthought it, and let a one-time invitation expire. This is the story, and the lesson about curiosity that stuck with me.*

Back in 2019, I was deep in the weeds of a debugging session, hopping between search results, stack traces, and documentation tabs. Somewhere between queries, the page split open. It wasn't an ad, and it wasn't a standard Google feature. The results folded back into a dark, UNIX-like terminal, and a single line blinked at me:

> Curious developers are known to seek out challenges...

Shortly after, a notification landed in my inbox from `noreply-foobar@google.com`: **"Google has a code challenge for you."**

![The foo.bar challenge email, sitting in my inbox since June 2019](/media/blog/google-foobar-email.png)

## 1. An Easter Egg in the Search Results

If you never encountered it, foo.bar was Google's legendary, semi-secret recruitment gauntlet. It was invitation-only, and the invitation was triggered by specific search patterns. Asking certain technical questions, the kind engineers ask when they are genuinely digging into a problem, could quietly summon it. It was a nod from the search engine that you were asking interesting questions.

The premise was delightfully absurd. You were tasked with rescuing The Bunny Planet from the clutches of Commander Lambda, one algorithmic challenge at a time. Behind the story sat real computer science: dynamic programming, graph traversal, number theory, each level tighter than the last.

The email came with a clear caveat, one that matters a lot in this story:

> NOTE: This is a one time use code. Please proceed when you're ready to play.

One time use. No second chance. No "I'll do it next weekend."

## 2. Why I Froze

I didn't enter.

Back then, imposter syndrome got the better of me. I convinced myself I wasn't ready, that my algorithmic chops weren't sharp enough, and that I needed more prep time before burning a single-use ticket from Google. So I did what many of us do with high-stakes opportunities: I overthought it. The terminal tab stayed open for a while, untouched. Then the window slipped away, and with it, the challenge.

For years, that expired invitation sat in my inbox as a small, quiet regret. I could have tried. Worst case, I would have failed a test case and learned something. Instead, I chose nothing, which is the only outcome that guarantees you learn nothing.

## 3. What It Taught Me

Here is the part I only understood later: letting that challenge sit idle taught me a far more enduring lesson than any solved test case would have.

The challenges you actually attempt teach you algorithms. The ones you avoid teach you about yourself. Mine pointed at a habit I have worked on ever since: hesitation dressed up as preparation. "I need more prep" is very often just fear of failure wearing a productive-looking costume.

## 4. Curiosity in the Age of Instant Answers

In an era where AI can synthesize answers in seconds and autocomplete entire functions, the daily habits of engineering feel different than they did in 2019. It is tempting to stop querying the moment an assistant hands you a plausible block of code. Accept, paste, move on.

But real breakthroughs, and the rare Easter eggs buried within the web, never come from passive acceptance. They belong to the people who keep digging, who inspect the network tab, who go five pages deep into obscure documentation, and who stay relentlessly curious after the first answer appears.

AI might give you quick answers, but curiosity gives you the right questions. foo.bar itself was proof: it only revealed itself to people who were already digging. The reward lived one query past the point where a less curious person would have stopped.

You never know what prompt, challenge, or hidden gem is waiting just past your next search.
